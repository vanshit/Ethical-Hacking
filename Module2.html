        02 · Mission Brief
        
What you will be able to do by the end

        
Six objectives, no jargon without a translation, and one non-negotiable rule: only investigate what you are authorised to test.

      

      🎯 Brief bonus: +45 XP
    


    

      

        

          Rules of engagement · read first
          
The line that separates a professional from a criminal

          
    
            
  • Ownership or permission. Search only systems, domains and data you own, or that you have written authorisation to test.
    • 
            
  • Public data is fine; intrusion is not. Reading a public webpage, DNS record or certificate log is like reading a noticeboard. Logging into someone's device is not.
    • 
            
  • Scope is written. A scope document says which domains, IP ranges, time windows and techniques are allowed — and which are not.
    • 
            
  • Hold, don't exploit. If you find a weakness, document it and report it. Do not prove it by breaking in.
    • 
            
  • No humans in the crosshairs. Reconnaissance is for systems. Profiling private individuals is harassment and is out of scope, always.
    •

        03 · Reconnaissance: The Big Picture
        
Reconnaissance is homework, not hacking

        
Reconnaissance (recon) is the information-gathering phase that happens before anything else. The goal is a map: what exists, who runs it, how it is built, and where the weak spots might be.

      

      Flip both cards to earn XP
    


    

      
      
    


    

      

        Passive · quiet by design
        

          
MethodWhat you get

          
WHOIS / RDAPRegistrar, key dates, nameservers, status codes

          
DNS lookupsPublic records: A, MX, NS, TXT

          
Certificate TransparencySubdomains revealed by public TLS certificates

          
Search engines / dorksIndexed pages, documents, forgotten staging sites

          
Shodan / CensysAlready-collected banners for exposed services

          
Social & press, job adsTechnology stack, vendors, org structure, timelines

        

        
◈Detectable? Usually no. You are reading copies of data that are already public — great for the first day of an engagement.

      

      

        Active · visible by design
        

          
MethodWhat you get

          
Port / service scansWhich ports answer, which software and versions

          
Direct DNS queries to target NSZone details; a misconfigured zone transfer can leak everything

          
HTTP probing / directory brute forceHidden paths, APIs, admin panels

          
Banner grabbingLive product names, versions, sometimes hostnames

          
Login / error probingWhether accounts exist (a sensitive line — scope carefully)

        

        
⚠Detectable and often illegal without permission. These requests land in the target's logs and can trip intrusion-detection systems. Never run them outside written authorisation.

      

    


    
⛔Same technique, different legality. A DNS query is legal against example.com because it is public infrastructure. The same query against a private hospital network without permission may violate computer-misuse law in your jurisdiction. Authorisation — not curiosity — decides.

    
Fun fact for context: reconnaissance is an official tactic in MITRE ATT&CK (TA0043 Reconnaissance) — defenders study it to predict where attacks begin.

  







  

    

      

        04 · WHOIS & RDAP Fundamentals
        
First stop on any domain: who registered it, and when?

        
WHOIS is the telephone directory of domain names. It answers: who registered it, who manages it, when it was created, when it expires, and which nameservers run it.

      

      Reference · no interaction needed
    


    

      

        Two protocols, one idea
        

          

            
WHOIS legacy

            
    
              
  • Port 43, plain text
    • 
              
  • Free-form output — every registrar formats it differently
    • 
              
  • ICANN sunset WHOIS for generic TLDs on 28 Jan 2025
    • 
            

          

          

            
RDAP current

            
    
              
  • Runs over HTTPS, returns structured JSON
    • 
              
  • Standard machine-readable fields (RFC 9082/9083)
    • 
              
  • Supports differentiated access — public vs. gated data
    • 
            

          

        

        
Practically: "do a WHOIS" now usually means "query RDAP". Tools, tutorials and CVs still use the old word. Source: ICANN, Jan 2025.

      

      

        Typical fields and what they tell you
        

        

          
FieldRead it as

          
RegistrarWhich company the domain was bought through — a pivot point to other domains using the same registrar pattern

          
Creation dateHow long the brand has existed. A famous brand on a 3-week-old domain is a phishing smell

          
Expiry datePayments overdue? Domains near expiry are sometimes forgotten and left unpatched

          
Updated dateRecent changes can hint at infrastructure migration or new ownership

          
NameserversWho hosts the DNS — a hosting provider, a CDN, or a self-hosted server

          
Status codesclientTransferProhibitedA registrar lock (EPP status). The domain cannot be transferred to another registrar while it is set — good hygiene against hijacking attempts. = registrar lock (good). pendingDelete = domain is dying

          
RegistrantOften redacted for privacy since GDPR and ICANN's 2025 Registration Data Policy — normal, not suspicious

          
Abuse contactThe correct address for reporting an incident — the professional way to escalate

        

        

      

    


    

      

        

          Why is the registrant often hidden? (privacy & proxy services)
          

            
Registrars act as a shield: the record shows the registrar or a privacy service instead of the real owner. This protects ordinary people from spam and harassment — and it also means redacted ≠ conspiracy.

            
Since ICANN's Registration Data Policy (2025), admin, billing and technical contact fields are no longer published for generic TLDs; only a limited set of data stays public.

          

        

        

          Limitations: what WHOIS/RDAP will not give you
          

            
    
              
  • Not a live security scan. It says who registered a name, not what is running on the server.
    • 
              
  • Not always accurate. It is self-declared data — outdated contacts are common.
    • 
              
  • Not complete. Country-code TLDs (.uk, .in, .de) follow their own registry rules and formats.
    • 
              
  • Not proof of ownership. A registrant record is not evidence that a person controls a server today.
    • 
            

          

        

      

      

        Defender's view
        
    
          
  • Brand protection. Watch for lookalike domains registered recently that copy your nameservers or trademark.
    • 
          
  • Asset inventory. If a domain in your inventory appears in a record you did not create, someone else is using your brand.
    • 
          
  • Expiry hygiene. Domains that expire silently break email and websites — and can be bought by someone else.
    • 
          
  • Correct abuse contact. Everyone wins when the abuse mailbox in the record is real and monitored.
    •

          What would a pentester notice? reveal
          

            
    
              
  • Old domain + many years of history → mature infrastructure, but also legacy systems nobody remembers.
    • 
              
  • Nameservers at a hosting provider → the provider manages DNS; look at their documented security posture rather than guessing.
    • 
              
  • Status lock present → good hygiene; transfer hijacking is harder.
    • 
              
  • Registrant redacted → expected. Do not try to "uncover" the owner; use the published abuse contact instead.
    •

    •

        06 · DNS Reconnaissance
        
DNS is the internet's address book — and your best map

        
DNS translates human names into machine answers. Every record type is a different clue: where the website lives, where email goes, who is in charge, and what a company has publicly admitted about itself.

      

      Public data · passive by default
    


    

      

        
        
        
        
        
        
      


      

        

          
What it is
A maps a name to an IPv4 address. AAAA does the same for IPv6. This is the record that puts a website on a server.

            
Example: www.example.com A 192.0.2.10

          
Why a security professional cares
    
            
  • Reveals hosting provider and rough geography of the server.
    • 
            
  • Shared IP = other sites on the same box. A neighbouring misconfigured site can affect yours.
    • 
            
  • An IP that is not behind the CDN everyone else uses is often a forgotten, unpatched origin server.
    • 
            
  • Compare with historical records: an IP that recently changed hints at migration and possible stale configurations.
    • 
          

        

      


      

        

          
What it is
An alias: shop.example.com simply points at another name, e.g. example.myshopify.com. Nothing is hosted locally.

          
Why it matters
    
            
  • Instantly tells you which third-party service powers that subdomain — a dependency and a supply-chain question.
    • 
            
  • Subdomain takeover risk: if the third-party service is deleted but the CNAME is left pointing at it, an attacker may be able to claim that name. Finding these is a classic passive-recon win — report it, don't claim it.
    • 
          

        

      


      

        

          
What it is
Mail eXchange records name the servers that accept email for the domain, with a priority number. Example: 10 mail.example.com

          
Why it matters
    
            
  • Shows whether email runs in-house (Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, a hosting provider) — huge for understanding an organisation.
    • 
            
  • An old or forgotten mail server in the list is a common weak point for spoofing and phishing filters.
    • 
            
  • MX + SPF + DMARC together tell you how well a domain protects its brand from email forgery. That is defensive intelligence, not an attack recipe.
    • 
          

        

      


      

        

          
What it is
Name Server records say who is authoritative for the zone — the organisations allowed to answer questions about it.

          
Why it matters
    
            
  • Identifies the DNS provider (Cloudflare, AWS Route 53, an ISP, an in-house server).
    • 
            
  • Nameservers are named meaningfully: ns1.eu.provider.net hints at where infrastructure lives.
    • 
            
  • Domains sharing unusual nameservers can be correlated — useful when mapping a brand's full estate or investigating lookalike domains.
    • 
            
  • A zone transfer (AXFR) against an authorised nameserver is an active technique: servers sometimes leak the entire zone. Only ever attempt it on systems you are authorised to test.
    • 
          

        

      


      

        

          
What it is
Free-text records that carry configuration and proof of ownership. Modern email security lives here, as SPF, DMARC and DKIM entries.

            
Example: v=spf1 include:_spf.example-mail.net -all

          
Why it matters
    
            
  • Reveals SaaS vendors and verification tokens: search-console proofs, analytics, chat widgets, ticketing systems — an inventory of the company's toolchain.
    • 
            
  • Missing or permissive email policy (~all, no DMARC) is a real, reportable finding about brand protection.
    • 
            
  • Because TXT records are public, never paste secrets into them. Leaked API keys in TXT have caused real incidents.
    • 
          

        

      


      

        

        

          
RecordMeaningRecon value

          
SOAStart of authority: primary NS, admin address, serial numbersWho to talk to; how often the zone changes

          
PTRReverse lookup: IP → nameTies an address back to naming conventions

          
CAAWhich certificate authorities may issue certs for the domainCertificate-hygiene check; missing CAA is a mild hardening gap

          
SRVService location (VoIP, LDAP, mail clients)Reveals services and internal naming style

          
DNSSECCryptographic signatures for DNS answersPassword for authenticating DNS; absence = spoofing is easier

        

        

      

    


    

      
Enterprise scale
One company may hold thousands of DNS records. Automated recon exists because nobody can read them by hand.

      
Pivots
Every record is a pivot: a name → an IP → a hosting provider → every other domain hosted there.

      
Certificate Transparency
TLS certificates are logged publicly. Subdomains listed in a certificate are readable at crt.sh even when they never appear in DNS search results.

    

  







  

    

      

        07 · Practical · DNS lookups on example.com
        
Query, read, interpret

        
DNS lookups are passive when you ask a public resolver about public records: you are reading a published directory, not knocking on a door.

      

      🛠 Activity: +25 XP

        Interactive · Find the signal
        
You are reviewing DNS findings for the fictional Aurora Logistics (aurora-logistics.example). Three findings are routine housekeeping. Which one deserves investigation first?

        

          
          
          
          
        

        

        

        

          Why B is the signal — and what a professional does next
          

            
A CNAME pointing to an unused third-party service is the classic setup for subdomain takeover: if that vendor name becomes claimable, someone else can serve content under the company's brand.

            
Authorised next steps: ask the client whether the vendor account still exists → check for a dangling record → remove or re-point the CNAME → verify with a DNS query (a harmless, data-only check). 

            
Never: register the abandoned name yourself, or serve content to "prove" the issue. That converts a finding into an incident.

          

        

      

    

  







  

    

      

        08 · Google Dorking · Advanced Search Operators · GHDB
        
Google dorking: the search engine as a reconnaissance tool

        
Google dorking — also called Google hacking — means using advanced search operators to cut millions of indexed pages down to the handful that matter. The community catalogue of those queries, grouped by what they expose, is the GHDB.

      

      🛠 5 questions: +50 XP
    


    

      

        

          ★ GHDB · Google Hacking Database
          
The public catalogue of Google dorks

          
The technique was named Google hacking after security researcher Johnny Long began cataloguing these queries in 2000. That catalogue is now hosted on Exploit-DB and maintained by OffSec (the makers of Kali Linux). Entries are organised by intent — for example:

          

            Footholds
            Files containing usernames
            Sensitive directories
            Files containing passwords
            Error messages
            Vulnerable files & servers
            Login portals
            Sensitive online shopping info
            Network or vulnerability data
            Various online devices
          

        

        

          
Read it like a defender. The GHDB is effectively a checklist of what your own site might be publishing by accident. Attackers treat it as a menu; professionals treat it as a to-do list for the domain they are responsible for.

          Open the GHDB ↗
          
exploit-db.com/google-hacking-database — free to browse, no account needed. It is a reference catalogue, not a scanner: it contains dork patterns, not exploits.

        

      

      
⛔Whose domain is it? Every query on this slide is written to be run against a domain you own or have written authorisation to test — that is why the examples use site:yourcompany.com. Running them against third parties and then following the results is not research; in most jurisdictions it is unauthorised access, even though the search engine did the indexing.

    


    

      

        Operator cheat sheet
        

        

          
OperatorMeaningSafe example

          
site:Only results from one site or domainsite:owasp.org

          
filetype: / ext:Only that kind of filesite:owasp.org filetype:pdf

          
intitle:Word must appear in the page titleintitle:"Swagger UI"

          
inurl:Word must appear in the URLinurl:login

          
intext:Word must appear in the page bodyintext:"SQL syntax near"

          
"exact phrase"Match that exact wording"aurora logistics"

          
-wordExclude a wordsite:example.com -inurl:blog

          
OR / ( )Either term; group conditionssite:example.com (login OR portal)

        

        

        
Reference: Google's own refine web searches page and Advanced Search.

      


      

        Legitimate uses you can start today
        
    
          
  • GHDB self-check. Open the GHDB, pick a category, and adapt the query to your own domain.
    • 
          
  • Your own asset inventory: site:yourcompany.com inurl:login — are there public login pages you did not know about?
    • 
          
  • Accidental exposure: site:yourcompany.com intitle:"index of" — is your web server still listing directories?
    • 
          
  • Document discovery: site:yourcompany.com filetype:pdf — which documents are public? Any that should not be?
    • 
          
  • Brand mentions: "yourcompany" -site:yourcompany.com — third-party pages and impersonators.
    • 
          
  • Learning: site:owasp.org filetype:pdf — free, authoritative security guidance.
    • 
        

        
⚠Operators change. Google retired the cache: operator in 2024 and now points to the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine for old versions of pages. Test your queries instead of trusting an old blog post.

      

    


    

      Dork patterns by vulnerability class · always scoped to a domain you own
      

      

        
What you are looking forExample dork (own-domain scoped)What it means — and what a defender does

        

          Exposed API docs
Swagger / OpenAPI		
          site:yourcompany.com (inurl:swagger-ui.html OR inurl:openapi.json OR intitle:"Swagger UI")
          Your API map — every endpoint, parameter and auth flow — is public. This is the highest-value finding on the list. Report it and take it behind authentication; do not probe the endpoints.
        

        

          Framework API specs
Spring Boot, ReDoc, /api-docs		
          site:yourcompany.com (inurl:/v3/api-docs OR inurl:/api-docs OR inurl:redoc)
          Auto-generated documentation that was never meant to be indexed. Remove it from production builds or protect it with authentication and monitoring.
        

        

          Database error leakage
SQL-injection indicator, not proof		
          site:yourcompany.com (intext:"SQL syntax near" OR intext:"You have an error in your SQL syntax")
          A page that prints raw database errors is leaking internals — a genuine finding. Document the leak and hand it to the owner. Testing the parameter behind it is active testing and needs written authorisation.
        

        

          Dynamic / parameterised URLs
GhDB's classic "vulnerable files"		
          site:yourcompany.com inurl:".php?id="
          A parameter in a URL is a candidate for authorised testing — never evidence of a bug by itself. Millions of parameterised pages are perfectly secure.
        

        

          Directory listings
          site:yourcompany.com intitle:"index of"
          Your web server is exposing a file tree. Fix: turn off auto-indexing, remove stray files, then re-request indexing removal.
        

        

          Backups & leftover files
          site:yourcompany.com (ext:bak OR ext:old OR ext:sql OR ext:zip)
          Files deleted-in-a-hurry but still live on the server. Remove them and review the deploy process that allowed them.
        

        

          Config & secrets
self-audit only		
          site:yourcompany.com (ext:env OR ext:ini OR ext:log)
          If this returns anything, treat it as an incident: rotate the exposed secrets, purge the file, and find out how it reached production.
        

        

          Admin & login portals
          site:yourcompany.com (inurl:login OR inurl:admin OR inurl:portal)
          Public login panels are normal — but each one must be patched, monitored and rate-limited. Decide whether it needs to be on the open internet at all.
        

        

          Staging / dev leftovers
          site:yourcompany.com (inurl:staging OR inurl:dev OR inurl:test)
          Non-production systems usually have weaker controls and stale data. Cross-check against your DNS and certificate inventory.
        

        

          Device interfaces
your own assets only		
          site:yourcompany.com (intitle:"camera" OR intitle:"router")
          Same lesson as Slide 11: inventory your devices and move them behind a VPN. Never interact with a device you do not own.
        

      

      

    


    

      

        Defender's GHDB workflow
        
    
          
  1. Scope it. Only your domains, subdomains and brand variants — write that scope down.
    2. 
          
  2. Run the categories most likely to hurt you: exposed API docs, directory listings, config files, staging hosts.
    3. 
          
  3. Triage every hit: public and intended, public and sensitive, or simply stale? Sensitive wins the priority list.
    4. 
          
  4. Fix at the source: remove the file, add authentication, correct the build — then request re-indexing and re-test.
    5. 
          
  5. Repeat monthly and after every big release. Indexed pages and cached copies linger longer than you think.
    6. 
        

      

      

        
⚠Discovery ≠ exploitation. Being able to see a page that a search engine indexed does not make accessing it authorised. If a result looks like private data, stop, do not open it, and report it to the site owner.

        
◈A parameter is a question, not a vulnerability. "This URL takes an id= parameter" is the start of an authorised test plan — never the end of an investigation, and never a reason to start poking a stranger's site.

      

    


    

  







  

    

      

        09 · OSINT Methodology
        
Six steps that turn random searching into intelligence

        
OSINT = Open-Source Intelligence: information from public sources, collected methodically and turned into something a decision can be made on. Click each step.

      

      🛠 2 challenges: +40 XP
    


    

      
      
      
      
      
      
    

    


    

      

        Mini-challenge · signal vs noise
        

      

      

        Field notes
        
    
          
  • One question per round. "Which vendors does Aurora use?" beats "find everything about Aurora".
    • 
          
  • Write scope down. Domains, time window, allowed techniques, and what you will do if you find something serious.
    • 
          
  • Correlate before concluding. Two weak clues agreeing with each other beat one dramatic claim.
    • 
          
  • Verify with a second source — and record when you could not.
    • 
          
  • Document as you go. Memory is not evidence; a timestamped note is.
    • 
          
  • Re-run later. OSINT has a shelf life. DNS records, staff and software all change.
    •

        11 · Shodan Dorks · Banner Grabbing · Exposure
        
A search engine for devices — and the technique behind it

        
Shodan scans the public internet and stores whatever services reply. That reply is a banner. This slide teaches the dorks that search those banners, how to grab a banner by hand on systems you are authorised to touch, what else you can read from a service, and what defenders do about exposed devices.

      

      Authorised scope only · discovery ≠ exploitation
    


    

      

        

          
          
          
          
          
        


        

          

            

              
How indexing works

              
    
                
  1. Shodan connects to publicly reachable IP addresses — a crawler, like Google's, but for services instead of pages.
    2. 
                
  2. It records the banner: whatever the service says back on connect.
    3. 
                
  3. It stores metadata alongside it: port, product, version, TLS certificate, ASN, country, and a screenshot where renderable.
    4. 
                
  4. You search that stored snapshot. You never touch the target — which is what makes it passive reconnaissance.
    5. 
              

            

            

              
What a result can show

              

                
IP / port
The address and the door that answered

                
Banner
Text or headers the service returned

                
Product / version
Software name and version string

                
Org / ASN
Who owns the network block

                
Location
Approximate country/city of the block — not a street address

                
Certificate
Hostnames, issuer, validity dates

                
Screenshot

A banner is small, but it is dense with useful, defensive information. Here is what an ethical hacker actually records from one.

          

            
TLS certificate details
Subject/SAN hostnames, issuer and validity dates → subdomains, which CA you use, and whether anything is about to expire.

            
HTTP headers
Server and framework strings, plus which security headers are missing (HSTS, CSP, X-Frame-Options) — a free hardening checklist.

            
Title & favicon hash
Names and fingerprints an application so you can find every instance of it — including ones nobody remembered deploying.

            
Authentication scheme
The WWW-Authenticate header says whether Basic, Bearer or OAuth is in play. That shapes an authorised test plan.

            
Protocol versions
SSH, TLS, FTP and SMTP version strings. TLS 1.0/1.1 still enabled is a hygiene finding worth reporting.

            
robots.txt & sitemap.xml
Paths the owner asked crawlers to skip. That is a request, not an access control — pair it with the dorking rules on Slide 08.

            
Default & vendor pages
Placeholder pages, "index of" listings and default product titles all say "nobody has finished configuring me".

            
Document metadata
Author names, internal paths and software versions hidden inside published PDFs and Office files. Strip yours before publishing.

          

          
◈And what a banner does not give you: no logins, no shell, no data. Reaching those means exploitation — which is outside this session, outside your authorisation, and outside the law in most countries.

        


        

          

            

              
Why cameras and IoT devices turn up exposed

              
    
                
  • Port forwarding at the router. Someone wants remote viewing, so they open a port to the internet and never close it.
    • 
                
  • UPnP auto-opening ports. Devices ask the router to expose themselves, and the router obliges.
    • 
                
  • Vendor "cloud P2P" modes that punch through NAT, so the app works away from home — sometimes with weak or shared protection.
    • 
                
  • Default or blank credentials on the web interface, or a stream that is not authenticated at all.
    • 
                
  • Firmware that never gets updated, so known flaws stay open for years.
    •

        14 · Staying Anonymous & Protecting Privacy Online
        
"Anonymous" is a spectrum, not a switch

        
Good OPSEC is not about dodging responsibility — it is about protecting yourself, your employer's data, and the confidentiality of your engagement.

      

      🛠 1 quick check: +15 XP
    


    
⚠Say this out loud: "Anonymous" does not mean "untraceable". Browser fingerprints, account logins, payment trails, timestamps, stylometry and DNS logs routinely de-anonymise people who assumed they were invisible. Anonymity tools reduce exposure — they do not grant invisibility.


    

      

        Browser & general privacy
        
    
          
  • Use a privacy-respecting browser and review its fingerprinting protections.
    • 
          
  • Separate profiles: work, personal, research. Never mix your real identity into a research profile.
    • 
          
  • Prefer a privacy-respecting search engine for sensitive queries.
    • 
          
  • Audit extensions — they see every page you visit.
    • 
          
  • Use a password manager and unique passwords; enable 2FA.
    • 
        

      

      

        VPN & Tor — what they actually do
        
    
          
  • A VPN hides your traffic from your local network and shifts trust to the VPN company. It is not anonymity: the provider, your bank, and logins you perform are still linked to you.
    • 
          
  • Tor routes traffic through multiple relays so no single hop knows both ends — at the cost of speed, and it does not protect you if you log into your own accounts while using it.
    • 
          
  • Never assume a tool makes an unauthorised action acceptable. Hiding your identity does not create permission.
    • 
        

      

      

        Metadata & account separation
        
    
          
  • Photos contain GPS and device data; documents carry author names and edit history.
    • 
          
  • Strip metadata before publishing anything from a lab or a client.
    • 
          
  • Research accounts get a separate alias email, unique avatar, and no cross-posting to personal profiles.
    • 
          
  • Remember: your writing style is a fingerprint too.
    • 
        

      

      

        Minimise personal exposure
        
    
          
  • Search yourself quarterly — see what the internet already knows about you.
    • 
          
  • Lock down social profiles; review tags and old posts.
    • 
          
  • Don't post live locations, employer badge photos, or boarding passes.
    • 
          
  • Use data-broker opt-outs where required by your local law; expect repeat requests.
    • 
        

      

      

        OPSECOperational Security: the everyday habits that prevent your own information from leaking — compartmentalised accounts, encrypted notes, minimal data collection, and thinking about who can see what. for professionals
        
    
          
  • Least exposure: never touch a client from a personal account or device you do not control.
    • 
          
  • Compartment: separate VMs/containers per engagement.
    • 
          
  • Log discipline: record what you ran and when — your defence if anyone asks.
    • 
          
  • Confidentiality: encrypt notes and evidence; share on a need-to-know basis.
    • 
          
  • Assume compromise: plan what you would do if your research laptop was lost.
    • 
        

      

      

        Legal reality check
        
Anonymity tools are legal in most places, but they do not change what is lawful. Using a VPN to scan a network you were never authorised to touch is still unauthorised access in jurisdictions such as the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the UK Computer Misuse Act, and India's IT Act. The tool is neutral; the permission is not.

        
Rules, residency and reporting duties differ by country — when in doubt, ask your instructor, your legal team, or the client in writing.

  2. Registration data and DNS are a map. WHOIS/RDAP tells you who and when; DNS and certificate transparency tell you what and where.
    3. 
          
  3. Method beats tooling. Question → Scope → Collect → Correlate → Verify → Document. The workflow is the skill; tools are just faster hands.
    4. 
          
  4. Signals are questions, not verdicts. An outdated banner or exposed panel means "investigate in an authorised test", never "attack now".
    5. 
          
  5. Ethics and documentation are professional skills. Scope, consent, minimising personal data, and clear written findings are what separate a trusted consultant from a liability.
    6. 
        

      


      

        Think like a defender
        
Everything in this deck cuts both ways. Ask the defender's version of each question:

        

          
Attacker's question
"What subdomains exist?"

          
Defender's question
"Have we inventoried every subdomain, and does each one still belong to us?"

          
Attacker's question
"What is exposed on our IP range?"

          
Defender's question
"Do we monitor our own ranges and alert on new open ports?"

          
Attacker's question
"Is the software outdated?"

          
Defender's question
"Do we have a patch policy with an owner and a deadline?"

          Final challenge · 60-second think
          
Your manager says: "A journalist emailed asking whether our systems are secure. Just do a quick scan of their website and their email setup so we know who we're dealing with."

          
          

            
✔A professional answer"I can review our own public footprint at any time — that is always in scope. Reviewing a journalist's infrastructure is a different matter: it needs a legitimate purpose, written authorisation from them (or a lawful basis), a defined scope, and a note of what data we retain about individuals. Without that, I'll gather only what is already public as part of our own brand-protection monitoring — and I will not probe their systems."

            
Key idea: purpose + permission + proportionality. If any of the three is missing, do not proceed.

⛔ Rules of engagement

      
    

    
This session teaches reconnaissance. The same knowledge can be misused, so here is the line, in plain language:

    
    
      
  • Authorised targets only: systems you own, systems you have written permission to test, purpose-built labs and CTF environments.
    • 
      
  • Public means public: reading public DNS, RDAP and certificate data is fine. Bypassing a login, guessing a password, or accessing a device that is not yours is not.
    • 
      
  • No live exploitation: this session never asks you to attack a real organisation.
    • 
      
  • No people-profiling: reconnaissance is for systems. Do not compile dossiers on private individuals.
    • 
      
  • Report, don't exploit: found something exposed? Report it to the owner or their abuse contact.
    • 
      
  • When unsure, stop and ask. "I was curious" is not a legal defence anywhere.
    • 
    

    
Legal duties differ by country and by contract. Your instructor, your employer and the client's scope document define what is allowed — this deck defines what is safe to practise.
